# Targeted implementation direction for an infinite procedural terrain engine

## Recommended direction

Given your requirements, I would not build version one as a fully volumetric world all the way to the horizon. The strongest fit is a **hybrid terrain system**: a **quadtree heightfield** for the global surface and very large draw distances, plus **sparse volumetric feature regions** for caves, overhangs, cliff cuts, and local digging. That recommendation follows directly from the trade-off in the literature: heightfields remain the most efficient way to render enormous terrains in real time, but they cannot represent caves or other volumetric features; voxel representations can represent those features, but they are heavier in memory, meshing, and LOD management. Hybrid approaches were proposed precisely to combine the efficiency of heightmaps with the expressiveness of voxels. citeturn9view0turn9view1turn19view0turn13view0

If I had to choose a concrete stack for you today, it would be this: **CDLOD for the far-field surface**, **OpenSimplex2S plus domain warping for the core procedural fields**, **ridged multifractal masks for mountain structure**, **a coarse climate simulation feeding a Whittaker-style biome lookup**, **Priority-Flood plus flow accumulation for river and erosion masks**, and **Transvoxel-meshed clamped density volumes for local 3D terrain and edits**. I would treat **Dual Contouring** as a later upgrade path rather than the first mesher: it is elegant and now has a very strong 2026 follow-on for sampled SDFs, but for a natural-terrain game where smooth topology, chunk LOD, and robustness matter more than sharp CAD-like edges, Transvoxel is the more battle-tested first implementation. citeturn9view1turn13view0turn14view0turn13view3turn13view4turn12view0turn11search0turn13view1turn9view7

Your intuition about “an infinite grid of octrees” is partly right, but I would narrow where octrees are used. Use a **quadtree** for the main surface pipeline, because most of the world is still fundamentally a surface problem, and the best large-scale terrain LOD methods are designed around that assumption. Use **octree-like or brick-hierarchical structures only for volumetric regions**, where they actually buy you caves, overhangs, and local edits. That split keeps the global renderer simple while reserving volumetric complexity for the places where it is visually valuable. citeturn9view1turn19view0turn13view0

## World representation and LOD

For the **global terrain surface**, I would pick **CDLOD** over geometry clipmaps as the primary implementation model. Geometry clipmaps are still foundational and excellent for nested regular-grid terrain rendering, but Strugar’s CDLOD is especially attractive for engine architecture because it keeps the terrain in a quadtree, selects nodes in a predictable way based on **three-dimensional distance**, and performs **per-vertex morphing** to smooth transitions. Strugar explicitly frames CDLOD as an improvement over basic clipmaps in cases where the observer height changes quickly and height-insensitive LOD becomes wasteful or visually unstable. That matches your need for very large draw distances and a streaming-friendly chunk hierarchy. citeturn9view0turn9view1

For **volumetric terrain**, use a **chunked density field** with **Transvoxel** for LOD stitching. The key reason is not just that Transvoxel makes smooth voxel terrain practical; it solves the exact problem that usually kills large voxel terrains: cracks at LOD boundaries. Lengyel’s method introduces transition cells between full- and half-resolution regions so multiresolution voxel terrain can be stitched seamlessly, and the official site plus MIT-licensed tables make it unusually implementation-friendly compared with many graphics papers. citeturn13view0turn13view5turn9view2

For **editable near-field terrain**, store a **clamped signed-distance-like density field**, not a mathematically exact infinite-range SDF. In practice, game terrain only cares about the neighbourhood around the surface, and clamping far-from-surface distances keeps modifications local and allows whole empty or solid chunks to collapse to uniform regions. The Voxel Tools documentation explains this point very clearly, and it aligns well with your requirement that digging be localised rather than globally propagating. citeturn14view0turn9view2

The hardest requirement in your brief is that **caves must be visible from a reasonable distance**. A pure “heightfield far away, voxel only nearby” system breaks down here, because a heightfield cannot show negative-space topology. My recommendation is therefore a **stratified hybrid**: the normal world is a heightfield, but any cell flagged as containing a cave or overhang also owns a **persistent low-resolution volumetric proxy** that remains active well beyond the editable radius. At long range, you can collapse that proxy to a very cheap “cave mouth” or subtractive opening representation if needed, but you should not rely on separate decorative cave meshes blended into the terrain, because the hybrid-terrain literature notes that this tends to create blending artefacts and constrains feature scale. That last part is an inference from the sources rather than a direct prescription, but it is strongly supported by the documented limitations of both pure heightfields and bolt-on cave meshes. citeturn19view0turn13view0

If you later decide that player-made cuts, structures, or mechanical excavation need much sharper edges than Marching Cubes-style extraction gives you, **Dual Contouring** is the natural upgrade path. The original 2002 paper showed real-time destructive modification on signed octrees using Hermite data, and the 2026 “Dual Contouring of Signed Distance Data” paper is particularly relevant because it removes the need for exact Hermite normals and reconstructs directly from sampled SDF data via local optimisation. That said, I would only take that path once the Transvoxel path is already shipping-quality. citeturn13view1turn9view7

## Noise and generation pipeline

Your terrain should not be “noise plus octaves plus done”. The modern lesson is that **the interesting part comes from how noise fields are combined, warped, remapped, and constrained by masks**, not from the base noise function alone. For the foundational field library, I would start with **OpenSimplex2S** and **FastNoiseLite**. OpenSimplex2 is explicitly positioned as a successor to older OpenSimplex variants, with fewer straight parts and better probabilistic symmetry, and FastNoiseLite gives you a portable implementation with OpenSimplex2, OpenSimplex2S, 2D/3D sampling, cellular noise, and built-in domain warp. citeturn8search8turn13view4turn13view3turn13view2

For **surface elevation**, use mostly **2D fields**. My recommended core stack is: a low-frequency base elevation field, a separate **mountainness** field, a **domain warp** field, and then biome-conditioned remapping. Use **ridged multifractal noise** for the mountain mask because it naturally produces ridge-like formations and is widely used for craggy terrain. After combining the fields, apply a **redistribution curve** rather than leaving the values linear; reshaping the value distribution is one of the simplest ways to get more plains, broader valleys, and more selective mountain ranges without changing the underlying frequency content. citeturn18view0turn13view3turn18view1

A practical first-pass elevation pipeline would look like this in design terms. Start with a low-frequency OpenSimplex2S surface field for broad relief, warp its domain with another low-frequency field so the world does not inherit obvious lattice alignment, multiply in a ridged-multifractal mountainness mask, and then apply separate remap curves for plains, hills, and mountains before blending them back together. Use **cellular or low-frequency region masks only for territory partitioning**—for example, deciding where mesa-like, alpine, or broken badlands terrain families are allowed—not as the direct source of terrain shape. That combination gives you deliberate control without falling back into smooth “blob world” behaviour. citeturn13view2turn13view3turn18view0turn18view1

For **3D cave fields**, use **3D OpenSimplex2S** sampled with the orientation recommended for terrain-like usage, and restrict it aggressively with masks. The OpenSimplex2 documentation specifically recommends the `ImproveXZ` orientation when the vertical axis is the odd one out, which is exactly your terrain case. More importantly, do not let 3D cave noise run everywhere. Gate it by depth below the surface, mountainness, rock type or biome, local fracture masks, and slope. This keeps the world from turning into Swiss cheese and lets caves concentrate beneath the kinds of terrain that visually justify them. citeturn8search17turn13view4

For **major cave systems**, pure 3D noise is not enough by itself if you want recognisable openings and traversable structure. Mark et al. explicitly note that pure fractal or noise approaches offer little control over desired features, and their cave-generation pipeline uses an L-system skeleton plus carving and curl-noise distortion to get more directed cave layouts. I would therefore make a distinction between **ambient caves** and **landmark caves**: ambient caves can be masked 3D noise, but landmark cave systems should be driven by a sparse spline, fracture graph, or grammar-guided tunnel skeleton that is then converted into subtractive density volumes. citeturn22view0turn21search1

## Climate, biomes, rivers and erosion

For biomes, a **simplified climate simulation** is the right choice for your project. AutoBiomes is a particularly useful reference here because it does almost exactly what you need: it computes biome distribution from a pipeline of **temperature**, **wind**, **precipitation**, and **biome classification**, and then uses a modified discretised **Whittaker diagram** as a lookup table from temperature-plus-precipitation to biome ID. It also models water as a moisture source and includes rain-shadow effects from terrain differences. That is much cheaper and more controllable than a full physical climate model, while still producing broad-scale coherence that simple independent noise maps usually lack. citeturn12view0turn12view2turn12view3

I would therefore run climate on a **coarse world grid** above the terrain system, not per render chunk. Temperature should come from latitude-like gradients plus elevation cooling; moisture should come from water proximity and atmospheric transport; precipitation should be advected by a small set of prevailing wind fields, with mountains creating rain shadows; and biome assignment should come from a table, not hard-coded if/else spaghetti. Because this grid is coarse and deterministic, it can drive not only terrain style but also textures, foliage densities, ambient colour, snowline, and cave permissiveness. citeturn12view2turn12view3turn12view1

For rivers and erosion, my recommendation is to keep the hydrology **simple but structurally important**. Start by hydrologically correcting your coarse DEM with **Priority-Flood**, which guarantees drainage and is widely used because it is simple and efficient. Then compute **flow direction** and **flow accumulation** on that corrected grid. RichDEM is a very practical reference implementation and supports both **D8** and **D∞** style flow metrics. citeturn11search0turn18view2turn18view3

Because rivers are not hero features in your brief, I would start with **D8** rather than D∞. D8 sends water to the steepest downslope neighbour and is still the standard simple extraction path for stream networks and contributing area. D∞ is smoother and reduces grid bias by distributing flow according to a continuous downslope direction over triangular facets, so it is a good future upgrade if you find the river masks too cardinals-and-diagonals looking. But for a first shipping terrain architecture, D8 gives you a clean, cheap signal for “where valleys and drainage want to form”, which is exactly what you need to drive erosion and valley carving. citeturn15search18turn18view2turn16search12turn16search2

I would then use hydrology in two ways. First, use flow accumulation to create **river masks**, **wetness masks**, and **valley-carving masks** that modify height, roughness, material selection, and vegetation density. Secondly, run a **light hydraulic-plus-thermal erosion pass** on the coarse terrain, either asynchronously during tile generation or as an offline bake for terrain-style presets. GPU erosion papers and professional terrain tools both reinforce the same idea: erosion is valuable not just because it changes height, but because it gives you secondary outputs such as flow, debris, sediment, wear, and deposition masks that are incredibly useful for texturing and biome dressing. citeturn10view4turn18view4turn18view5turn18view6

One useful modern extra is to reserve a later “polish” phase for **procedural erosion detail**, rather than simulating everything physically. Recent work on real-time terrain enhancement with controlled procedural patterns shows that erosion-like relief detail can be added in real time with artist control. I would not make this a first-pass dependency, but it is a smart later-stage tool if your base world reads well at macro scale and you simply want more convincing micro-relief without heavier simulation. citeturn17search0turn17search9

## Editing, authoring and debugging tools

The first authoring tool I would build is a **terrain function graph inspector**, even if the shipping generator is code-first. Voxel Plugin’s graph system and Voxel Tools’ procedural-generation docs both underline the same practical truth: terrain generators evaluate huge numbers of samples, so iteration speed matters as much as raw algorithm choice. You want a way to inspect every intermediate field—base elevation, warp vector, mountainness, cave mask, temperature, moisture, precipitation, flow accumulation, erosion wear, deposition, biome ID—without recompiling the world every time. citeturn13view8turn13view7

For voxel terrain specifically, I would consider four debug views non-negotiable. You need a **density slice viewer** for inspecting the 3D field directly, an **LOD seam visualiser** for transition boundaries, a **chunk residency view** for streamed and edited regions, and a **material/biome overlay** showing the classification result independently of the final shading. Those tools are not just “nice to have”: the referenced climate and erosion pipelines are multi-stage systems with important intermediate products, and the easiest way to waste months on procedural terrain is to only look at the final shaded mesh. citeturn12view2turn12view3turn18view6

For **external authoring**, I would treat **Houdini HeightFields**, **Gaea**, and **World Machine** as reference laboratories rather than runtime dependencies. Houdini’s HeightField Erode explicitly supports chained erosion at multiple scales and gives you flow and sediment-style layers; Gaea frames erosion as central to believable terrains; World Machine emphasises both erosion shaping and the value of wear, deposition, and flow masks for texturing. These tools are ideal for learning what good parameter ranges look like, for producing target exemplars per biome, and for authoring preset libraries even if your final world is generated entirely in-engine. citeturn18view4turn18view5turn18view6

For **sparse volumetric tooling**, keep **OpenVDB/NanoVDB** on your radar, but probably not as your core runtime data structure in version one. The official OpenVDB project has continued to improve NanoVDB’s GPU workflow, including tools to construct and modify NanoVDB grids on the GPU. That makes it potentially useful as an interchange or inspection format for sparse volumes, especially if you want to export cave fields to DCC tools or inspect them outside the game. citeturn13view6

On the shading side, plan early for **triplanar materials** and **far-distance normal/detail augmentation** on the voxel meshes. Voxel Tools documents both triplanar mapping for arbitrary smooth voxel surfaces and a detailed-normal approach for medium and far LODs so distant overhangs keep visual richness without exploding triangle count. The same principle applies to your heightfield renderer: geometry carries macro shape, while normals and material masks carry a lot of the apparent detail at range. citeturn14view0turn9view0

## Suggested build order and asset requirements

The right order is to lock the **macro structure first**, then add topology, then add editability, then add art richness. I would follow this sequence.

1. **Ship the far-field surface renderer first**: implement deterministic macro-tiles, quadtree addressing, and a CDLOD-based heightfield renderer with only surface elevation and basic materials. Until this exists, every later system will be judged in the dark because you will not know whether a problem is procedural design or renderer instability. citeturn9view1turn9view0

2. **Build the procedural field stack next**: OpenSimplex2S base relief, domain warp, mountainness masks, and redistribution curves. This is the stage where you stop producing “glorified sine waves” and start producing controllable terrain families. Use FastNoiseLite for rapid iteration and a single seedable field framework. citeturn13view3turn13view2turn13view4turn18view0turn18view1

3. **Add climate and biome classification before caves**: a coarse temperature–wind–precipitation pipeline and a biome lookup table should come before 3D terrain, because they decide where mountains, snow, deserts, forests, swamps, karstic cave zones, and vegetation distributions are even allowed to exist. Without this layer, every later cave or erosion choice becomes globally noisy instead of regionally meaningful. citeturn12view0turn12view2turn12view3

4. **Add hydrology and erosion masks next**: Priority-Flood, D8 flow direction/accumulation, stream thresholding, wetness, and a light erosion pass. Even if rivers never become a marquee feature, this stage pays for itself because it gives you valleys, drainage-aligned detail, believable material placement, and better biome transitions. citeturn11search0turn18view2turn10view4turn18view6

5. **Only then add sparse volumetric feature chunks**: start with masked 3D cave noise plus Transvoxel meshing and low-resolution far proxies for cave regions. Do not begin with player editing. First prove that the world can stream cave topology, render it at multiple LODs, and show cave mouths at distance without cracks. citeturn13view0turn14view0turn19view0

6. **Layer local digging and sculpt edits after volumetric LOD is stable**: use clamped density brushes, chunk-local regeneration, and an edit journal over the procedural base. This is where the chunked-density representation and local regeneration requirements from voxel terrain literature become critical. citeturn14view0turn9view2turn13view1

7. **Finish with materials, foliage, and landmark authoring**: triplanar biome materials, erosion-driven masks, procedural asset placement, and a small authored system for landmark cave skeletons or entrances. AutoBiomes is a good reference for how biome simulation and asset placement reinforce one another. citeturn12view1turn14view0turn22view0

In asset terms, the minimum viable set is not primarily meshes; it is **data**. You need a **biome lookup table** from temperature and precipitation to biome ID, **per-biome material rules** that work with triplanar projection, **erosion-derived masks** for flow, wear, and deposition, **vegetation placement rules** keyed by biome and wetness, and **terrain-style presets** that bundle noise, climate, and erosion parameters into named terrain families. If you want standout caves rather than just ambient holes, add a small library of **authored cave skeleton templates** or entrance masks so landmark volumes are guided rather than fully random. citeturn12view2turn12view1turn18view6turn14view0turn22view0

The short version is this: **surface first, climate second, hydrology third, sparse 3D features fourth, digging fifth**. That order best matches your priorities of variation, large draw distance, infinite scale, and a moderate but meaningful amount of 3D topology. It also keeps the implementation risk where it belongs: concentrated in the volumetric regions that justify it, rather than spread across the whole planet-sized world. citeturn19view0turn9view1turn13view0
